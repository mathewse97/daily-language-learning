# ENGLISH_COURSE — professional English for design work

> Metalanguage for this track is English (invariant 6). The course document itself stays
> in English for the same reason.

## What this track is actually for

His receptive English is already strong. Reading comprehension is not the bottleneck and
should not be treated as one. The three real gaps are:

1. **Collocation** — knowing that you *make* a case, *raise* a concern, *walk someone
   through* a rationale, *push back on* a brief. Wrong collocations are what make fluent
   non-native English sound off.
2. **Register control** — the same idea said to a client, to a teammate in Slack, and in
   a published case study are three different sentences. He needs all three.
3. **Domain knowledge itself** — the track should teach him something real about design,
   not just wrap vocabulary in a design-flavoured wrapper.

So: no isolated vocabulary lists, ever. Target language appears inside a text worth
reading on its own merits, and leaves through his own production.

## Register rotation

No phases — a rotation, weighted toward client work.

| Register | Share | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **Client-facing** | ~50% | Proposals, defending decisions, scoping, pricing conversations, presenting rationale. Hedged, confident, structured. |
| **Async team** | ~30% | Slack, design critique, specs, handoff notes, feedback on someone's work. Concise, direct, collaborative. |
| **Public writing** | ~20% | Case studies, articles, talk abstracts, portfolio copy. Polished, narrative, first person. |

Rotate so the *same* topic recurs across registers over time. Explaining type hierarchy
to a client, to a developer, and in a case study is the exercise that makes the
vocabulary transferable rather than inert.

## Topic pool

Typography and type systems · visual hierarchy and scanning · brand identity and brand
systems · design tokens and theming · design systems governance · component API design ·
UI patterns and their tradeoffs · interaction and motion · accessibility (WCAG, contrast,
focus order, screen readers) · responsive and fluid layout · information architecture ·
UX research methods · usability testing · service and journey mapping · design critique
practice · creative direction · marketing and campaign design · conversion and landing
pages · product strategy and discovery · design ops · Framer and no-code production ·
AI-assisted design workflows · design–engineering handoff · portfolio and case-study
craft · pricing and scoping creative work · client relationships and difficult
conversations.

## Packet format (~13 min, Tue/Thu/Sat)

1. **The text** — 180–250 words, written as a real piece of professional writing in the
   day's register, not as a lesson. It must teach him something true about the topic. If
   it makes a factual claim about a standard, a tool, or a study, it must be accurate;
   when unsure, write around it rather than assert.

2. **In context** — 5–7 target items pulled from the text, each shown as the *phrase*,
   not the word, with a one-line gloss in English and a note on register where it matters.

   > **above the fold** — the part of a page visible before scrolling.
   > Fine with clients, slightly dated in product teams.

   Collocations and multi-word chunks always beat single words. `visual hierarchy`,
   `scanability`, `above the fold` — never `hierarchy = hierarquia`.

3. **Check** — 2 questions on the substance of the text, not on the vocabulary.

4. **Say it** — the core of the track, done in his head. One situation, a named register,
   2–3 required target items, and an instruction to compose two or three sentences
   mentally before opening the model. Nothing is written or typed.

5. **One expression** — a single high-value professional phrase per packet, with the
   situation it belongs to and one situation where it would be wrong.

6. **Key** — answers, plus a model version of the writing task and three specific things
   to compare against in his own draft (not "check your grammar" — "did you hedge the
   recommendation, or state it as fact?").

The matching data fields are in `WEEK_PACKET_SCHEMA.md`, card `lang: "en"`.

## Correction policy

He composes mentally and self-checks against the model. Since there is no draft to mark,
the key is doing all the work: name the two or three decisions the model answer made —
hedging, sentence length, where the recommendation sits — so he can hold his own version
against them. A model answer with no stated decisions teaches nothing.

If he replies to a packet in a live session with his own writing, correct it precisely:
collocation and register first, grammar second, and never rewrite the whole thing when a
two-word fix does the job. Preserve his voice; the goal is his English, not yours.

## What to log

In `state/lexicon.md`, section `EN-COLLOC`: the phrase, its register, and the
date. These re-enter through the same spaced-repetition queue as everything else — a
phrase comes due and must appear in a later text, ideally in a *different* register from
where he first met it.

> The `data` column of that section is ambiguous: entries have carried dates in the
> future, which suggests it records the delivery date rather than the date the phrase
> was met. It is recorded as unresolved in `LEXICON_SCHEMA.md` and has not been
> reinterpreted.
